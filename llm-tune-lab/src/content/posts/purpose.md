Ever since the release of GPT-3 in 2020, the enterprise world has increasingly been changing its trajectory to try to align their businesses with the breakthrough presented by OpenAI. Earlier sequence models, such as recurrent neural nets and long-short term memory models, struggled to produce more than a coherent sentence or two. GPT-2, whose largest variant had 1.5 billion parameters, showed that Transformer-based language modeling could produce significantly more coherent long-form content. Following the hypothesis that a larger model with more data and more compute would perform better, OpenAI scaled GPT-3 up to 175 billion parameters. Now fast forward to 2026 and open-weight models have started to exceed 1 trillion parameters, such as Kimi-K2.5 (1.1T params) [1] and DeepSeek-V4-Pro (1.6T params). Considering the exponentially larger financial backing of closed-weight top AI labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic, it is plausible, but not confirmed, that GPT-5.5, Claude Opus, and Claude Mythos are in a size class above these, potentially sitting around 5 trillion parameters. To put that into perspective, aggressivety quantized with an average of 3 bits per weight, that would require ~1750 GB of space just for the weights. The most common server deployment is currently a rack of 4 DGX-class systems which each contain 8x80GB H100 GPUs for roughly 2560 GB of RAM. This means the model weights alone consume 2/3 of the entire hyperscaler rack before considering KV-Cache memory overhead, routing overhead, activations, batching, and long-context requests. A single one of these racks usually costs several million dollars without factoring in maintennance and power. Additionally, training requires a massive amount of compute which results in billions of dollars of losses before the model can be put on the server to start generating revenue through the service.

## Frontier LLM Progress

Early transformer LLMs, despite being a massive step up from previous architectures, were still admittedly terrible. They would blatantly hallucinate, get stuck in loops where they could only come up with two incorrect solutions and iterate between the two, lose conversational context, and worst of all, they would make facts up. Today, they are objectively much better (in most domains). Disregarding benchmarks for a moment and focusing on anecdotal experience, Chain-of-Thought demonstrated one of the biggest jumps in LLM ability. Switching from using GPT-4o to o1 from OpenAI was like night and day. By training the model to deliberately reason through how to solve a problem before attempting it, the answer trajectory has become significantly more accurate and does not stray. The next big jump was tool calling (and hence the rise of what is known as Agentic AI). During the post-training phases (Supervised Finetuning and/or Reinforcement Learning), models are being trained to call tools and use the results as additional context to continue generating tokens. This can be tools such as reading files or sandbox environments. This allows a model to write functions and test them repeatedly, log errors, fix its work, and run tests before committing to a solution meaning that the risk of an error is significantly lower. 

There have been many architectural changes and improvements, however, interestingly they appear to be different depending on the country of origin due to the unique constraints in each. Frontier labs from the United States have a focus on "acheiving Artificial General Intelligence". Because of the hundreds of billions of dollars being invested, these labs are incentivized to make ambitious promises to continue to secure funding. That means ensuring that the next model does better than the its competitor's latest model on the benchmarks and product integration. We don't know the exact architectural changes, but the reasonable assumption is more data, more compute, more RL, and more parameters. Chinese labs, on the other hand, tend have an differing strategy. Their goal is not to surpass the frontier labs in the US, but rather to create models that are top 3-5 on the benchmarks and are 100x cheaper to run inference on. Chinese labs have nowhere near the same funding or compute resources compared to their US counterparts, so they have to be strategic with their development. A perfect example of this is the latest DeepSeek-V4-Pro model. Despite being a 1.6 trillion parameter model, it is heavily optimized for server inference. They have innovated new architectures for the attention mechanisms designed specifically to reduce long-context costs. It is certainly the most complex open-weight architecture out there where they use Compressed Sparse Attention which is a combination of DeepSeek sparse attention (compute full attention in low precision, select the top-k most relevant scores, then re-run attention only for those tokens in full precision) in tandem with compressed KV values from Heavily Compressed Attention. According to their results, at a 1M token context, it only need a KV-Cache 10% the size required by their previous state-of-the-art model DeepSeek-V3.2 Terminus which was already a very efficient model.

## Enterprise Adoption

Ironically, despite what the benchmarks and CEOs claim the capabilities of these models are, we have yet to see concrete economic productivity gains from LLMs. We have been hearing "Software Engineering is dead in 6 months" for 5 years now, almost always coming from non-technical individuals or CEOs with direct financial incentive to promote AI adoption. Currently, the main users of LLMs are software engineers and students. The main adoption, therefore, has been by software companies. Many of these companies are using AI to automate repetitive workflows, speed up development, generate reports from data, etc. There has also been a rise in "AI Wrapper" startups: companies whose products are built primarily around API calls to a frontier model wrapped in a client-facing workflow or interface.Examples include tools like Perplexity, Cluely, and Cursor, although Cursor has added deeper into integration than the other two. There has also been a push to encourage or mandate employees to use AI, sometimes by implementing metrics tracking or productivity initiatives. In the worst cases, AI adoption is forced as a theater for investors to demonstrate being an "AI-first company" and not for the sake of productivity.

## Prediction

With a company focused on AI, being realistic is the first and most important part. The current state of AI can not be profitable. The costs of operating these behemoth class models are simply infeasible. For the last few years, investors have subsidized the true cost of running these models. It is the same situation as when Uber came out and rides were $5 and now that same ride is $50. We have already seen the cracks start showing in 2026. Anthropic has publicly apologized following secretly capping thinking tokens in Claude Code because they didn't have the available compute and are now reportedly removing almost all of the benefits of their unlimited $200 a month plan. Some people are now realizing their API bill, if continuing the same level of use, is going to be thousands, if not tens of thousands. OpenAI's latest API prices are also rising. Just for these companies to break even, they need to bill hundreds of dollars per million output tokens, 10x+ higher than they currently are. They know it's unsustainable which leads to this warning: Do NOT build critical infrastructure that requires frontier level AI capabilites. Creating and serving frontier AI models is usually not financially profitable, however, creating the tools and wrappers around them has shown to be. When Cursor and Perplexity came out, they were some of the fastest companies to reach certain valuations and Cursor being the fastest to reach a 1 Billion dollar evaluation in history. The problem is that the moment the AI providers sniffed profits, they immediately created teams to develop competing products which they could then subsidize to give users an incentive to come to them. Cursor ended up training their own model because they realized they wouldn't be able to compete until they had one that was their own, but couldn't afford the server costs and are now in a potential merged with SpaceX/Grok. With many companies having written so much infrastructure using AI (whether it is a product that completely relies on one of these LLMs or a massive codebase generated by AI), the technical debt is so massive that large corporations will opt to pay the reflective AI prices because they can't go afford to go back. When a company like Microsoft cancels their partnership with Anthropic because it is costing them too much money, that really puts into perspective the harsh reality about the direction of AI usage.

For AI to be profitable and useful, we likely need to stop relying on general-purpose behemoth models and move towards lightweight LLMs specialized for specific tasks through LoRA SFT or RL. As a developer, you do not need a 1 trillion parameter model that can recite Harry Potter line for line in 50 languages and know every Wikipedia fact ever recorded. You need a model adapted to your specific tasks and environment. If you work in Java and Spring Boot, you need a model well-versed in this version of Java and Spring Boot. If you work in a legacy C++ codebase, you don't need a model that can code OCamL or COBOL, you need a model highly competent in C++ and those programming concepts that can follow your programming patterns without getting lost. For business users, the solution should not be Claude Opus with a massive Copilot harness in Excel, it should be a lightweight model trained specifically on how and when to solve tasks by calling functions on your behalf. Rather than trying to create general trillion parameter models, the industry should be trying to create the infrastructure to easily create millions of 10 billion parameter specialized models. With models small enough to run on a Mac or gaming laptop at a reasonable speed, true economic gains are much more possible and realistic.

Our work is focused on small AI models and making them moree efficient. This includes curating datasets for specific tasks, training adapters for specific use-cases, writing our kernel libraries that make LoRA training practical on less powerful hardware, or experimenting on existing lightweight LLMs to make them more useful. We hope to publish findings that lead to cheaper, more efficient, more useful, and ultimately better AI for people and the plant.
